The WHO-ICRC Basic Emergency Care participant workbook describes the ABCDE approach as a framework for the systematic and organized evaluation of acutely ill patients in order to rapidly identify and intervene for life-threatening conditions. A stands for Airway: check for and correct any obstruction to movement of air into the lungs. B stands for Breathing: ensure adequate movement of air into the lungs.

In the ABCDE approach, C stands for Circulation: evaluate whether there is adequate perfusion to deliver oxygen to the tissues, and check for signs of life-threatening bleeding. D stands for Disability: assess and protect brain and spine functions. E stands for Exposure: identify all injuries and any environmental threats and avoid hypothermia. The ABCDE approach is a stepwise approach designed to ensure that life-threatening conditions can be identified and treated early, in order of priority.

If a problem is discovered in any of these steps, it must be addressed immediately before moving on to the next step. The ABCDE approach should be performed in the first 5 minutes and repeated whenever a patient's condition changes or worsens. For the airway assessment, the workbook asks whether the patient can talk normally. If yes, the airway is open.

If the patient cannot talk normally, look to see if the chest wall is moving and listen to see if there is air movement from the mouth or nose. Listen for abnormal sounds such as stridor, grunting, or snoring, or a hoarse or raspy voice that indicates a partially obstructed airway. Stridor plus swelling or hives suggests a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis).

In the glossary of the Basic Emergency Care workbook, shock is a state where organs do not get enough blood and oxygen (poor perfusion), leading to organs not working properly. Perfusion is the delivery of blood to body tissues. Hypoxia is low levels of oxygen in the blood. Hypotension is blood pressure lower than the normal range. Tachycardia is a heart rate faster than normal range, and tachypnoea is rapid breathing.

The Basic Emergency Care glossary defines the Glasgow Coma Scale as a system of assessing the neurologic function of a trauma patient. It is a score ranging from 3 (unresponsive) to 15 (normal) that assesses responsiveness based on eye movement, verbal response and motor response. The level of consciousness describes the level of responsiveness or alertness to the environment. Lethargy is excessive drowsiness and slowness to respond.

According to the Basic Emergency Care glossary, difficulty in breathing, sometimes also called shortness of breath, can result from many causes, including problems with the lungs, problems with oxygen, airway blockage, fast breathing, and weak respiratory muscles. Nasal flaring, the widening of nostrils during breathing, results from increased effort and is a sign of difficulty in breathing.

Retractions, the visible pulling in of tissues between the ribs or around the collarbones with strained inspiration, are a sign of serious difficulty in breathing. The glossary also states that stridor is a high pitched sound on breathing in that is caused by swelling or a physical obstruction of the upper airway.

A seizure, also called convulsions or fits, is abnormal electrical activity in the brain, often seen as altered mental status with abnormal repetitive movements. A heart attack, also called myocardial infarction, is death of heart muscle due to a lack of oxygen-rich blood getting to the heart. Hypoglycaemia is low blood sugar. A pulse oximeter is a device that detects oxygen saturation, the percentage of red blood cells saturated with oxygen.
